\begin{abstract}
% DRAFT abstract, written from the framework text; revise before submission.
Conservation programs usually measure success as a drop in withdrawal at the point of use. River basins, however, respond to changes in depletion and to the timing and location of return flows. We propose one parameterized framework that links the two. We use a change identity to divide any reported saving into a ``wet'' part that reduces depletion and a ``dry'' part that only removes return flow. We then add a fate equation for what happens to the freed water (protection, local reuse, transfer, or banking) and a routing equation that places the resulting gains and losses in space and time. Four basic types of conservation follow from the parameter signatures, and the terms of the framework map directly onto two-ledger Sankey diagrams. In worked examples, the practice with the largest reported saving delivers the smallest net savings and, under local reuse, increases depletion. The framework also identifies when conservation harms third parties downstream: when a practice removes return flow that others rely on, when freed water is consumed again, and when lost return flow arrives later or farther downstream than the gains.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
% ===========================================================================

Conservation programs usually count success as a drop in withdrawal at the point of use. A river basin responds to something else. It responds to the change in depletion, meaning water evaporated, transpired, exported, or sent to a sink. It also responds to shifts in return flows across space and time. The drop in withdrawal and the change in depletion can differ in size, and even in sign. When they differ, the difference falls on someone else: downstream users, groundwater pumpers, and stream reaches that relied on the return flow, or on water that a practice now consumes.

Evidence for this gap comes from basin models, field data, economic theory, and urban climate studies. \citet{ward2008} modeled the Upper Rio Grande and found that subsidies for efficient irrigation can increase depletion. \citet{pfeiffer2014} used panel data from western Kansas and found that conversion to more efficient center pivots increased groundwater extraction. \citet{contor2013} used an economic model to show why this outcome is the general case when irrigators behave rationally, a point \citet{huffaker2003} made earlier on theoretical grounds. \citet{grafton2018}, in a multi-author synthesis, called this pattern the irrigation-efficiency paradox and called for full water accounting. The gap also arises outside agriculture and outside the annual water balance. In the Walla Walla Basin, \citet{scherberg2018} found that piping canals over a connected aquifer reduced modeled groundwater storage unless managed aquifer recharge (MAR) replaced the lost seepage. Replacing urban lawns reduces consumptive use but raises daytime temperatures \citep{vahmani2016,saher2022}.

Washington law already draws the key distinction, but practice is less settled. The law defines ``net water savings'' as water conserved and usable in specified reaches without impairing existing rights \citep{rcw9042020}. A recent review of conservation in eastern Washington \citep{wadraft2026} found that programs mostly estimate savings at the point of use, report them in aggregate, and seldom trace them to the source. The review also found that agricultural and municipal programs run overlapping practices without coordinating on basin outcomes.

Our objective is to build one quantitative framework that traces the water a practice frees to basin depletion and to the timing and location of river flows. Section~\ref{sec:levers} sets out five levers through which a practice changes the fate of water. Section~\ref{sec:identity} develops the framework. A change identity splits any reported saving into a wet part, which reduces depletion, and a dry part, which only removes return flow. A fate equation follows the freed water, a routing equation places gains and losses in space and time, and a shortfall measure tracks water availability. Section~\ref{sec:parameters} lists the parameters that specify a practice, and Section~\ref{sec:typology} derives a typology of four basic types and four dispositions. Section~\ref{sec:visual} proposes two-ledger Sankey diagrams to display the results, and Section~\ref{sec:examples} works through illustrative irrigation and municipal cases. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} draws on these results to identify the circumstances in which conservation harms third parties. Section~\ref{sec:next} sets out next steps, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes. Table~\ref{tab:definitions} in the appendix defines the paper's terms in plain language, with their notation.

The paper makes three contributions. The first is a single change identity that accounts for the effect of any conservation practice, in any sector, on basin depletion. The water-accounting literature partitions a use into consumed and non-consumed water, beneficial and non-beneficial consumption, and recoverable and non-recoverable flow \citep{molden1997,perry2007}, and the International Commission on Irrigation and Drainage recommends this terminology at every scale \citep{perry2009}. \citet{karimi2013} build these fractions into Water Accounting Plus (WA+), a set of basin accounting sheets derived from remote sensing and models. Both describe the state of a system; neither is designed to attribute a change to a practice or to compare practices. \citet{seckler1996} distinguishes wet savings, which reduce depletion, from dry savings, which only remove water that would have returned, but draws the distinction conceptually. Our change identity (Equations~\eqref{eq:change} and~\eqref{eq:freed}) recasts the accounting fractions as changes between states and computes the wet and dry parts of any reported saving. Because the resulting typology rests on these parameters rather than on technologies, it explains why the same hardware yields a wet saving in one setting and a dry saving in another. The terms of the identity can also feed WA+ sheets at the basin scale.

The second contribution is an explicit account of what happens to the water a practice frees. The literature on the irrigation-efficiency paradox documents and explains how efficiency gains can raise depletion: \citet{ward2008} in basin models of the Upper Rio Grande, \citet{pfeiffer2014} with panel data from western Kansas, and \citet{contor2013} with an economic model of irrigator behavior. \citet{grafton2018} synthesize this work and call for full water accounting, consumption caps, and risk assessment. These studies diagnose the mechanism by which freed water is consumed again, but they offer no accounting form that applies across practices and destinations of the freed water. The paracommons literature, which examines who gains from a saving \citep{lankford2013}, comes closest: \citet{lankford2023} develop a spreadsheet model that reallocates irrigation savings among the proprietor, neighbours, nature, and society, with nature and society as lumped residuals. Our fate equation (Equation~\eqref{eq:fate}) gives each destination an explicit depletion fraction, in any sector. Local reuse becomes a single term, and the paradox becomes a testable sign condition: depletion rises when the reused part of the freed water consumes more than the wet saving. Empirical studies such as \citet{pfeiffer2014} could report results against that condition.

The third contribution is to place the gains and losses from conservation in space and time and to link them to the third parties who bear them. \citet{lankford2023} compare a baseline with future scenarios at steady state, without seasonality, lags, or reach-by-reach routing, and \citet{lankford2020} treats scale through a qualitative matrix of five spatial scales and ten dimensions of irrigation efficiency. Our routing equation (Equation~\eqref{eq:routing}) follows each change in diversion and return flow to every reach and month, with lags set by a transfer kernel. Our shortfall measure (Equation~\eqref{eq:shortfall}) then turns the resulting changes in flow into changes in water availability. Together they identify when a practice that reduces basin depletion still harms downstream users, and they give a physical counterpart to the Washington test that net water savings not impair existing rights \citep{rcw9042020}. Program reporting now stops at the point of use \citep{wadraft2026}; these measures would extend it to the places and seasons where conservation has its effects.

\section{How a practice can change the fate of water}\label{sec:levers}
% ===========================================================================

A conservation practice can change the fate of water through five levers (Table~\ref{tab:levers}). Some levers do not show up as a drop in withdrawal. Most real practices pull more than one lever at once, and we build the framework in Section~\ref{sec:identity} to track all five.

Two consequences shape the equations that follow. First, \emph{observed withdrawal is an unreliable indicator}. Withdrawal can fall with no change in depletion, when lever~2 removes only return flow. Withdrawal can also stay flat while depletion rises, when local reuse under lever~3 follows lever~2. The equations therefore track the change in every part of the water balance, not withdrawal alone. Second, \emph{some practices act on water that is never withdrawn}. Rainwater harvesting, cover crops, and land-cover change alter evapotranspiration (ET) of precipitation. The framework handles them by replacing withdrawal with total water input, withdrawal plus effective precipitation, throughout. This substitution matches the distinction in basin water accounting between ET supplied by precipitation and incremental ET supplied by withdrawals \citep{karimi2013}.

\section{The foundational identity}\label{sec:identity}
% ===========================================================================

Any practice changes the components of the water balance of a use. Equation~\eqref{eq:partition} partitions a use. Equations~\eqref{eq:change} and~\eqref{eq:freed} account for the change a practice makes and separate the part the basin feels from the part it does not. Equation~\eqref{eq:fate} adds what happens to freed water, Equation~\eqref{eq:routing} places the effects in space and time, and Equation~\eqref{eq:shortfall} turns changes in flow into changes in water availability.

\paragraph{Partition of use.} Take one use within a defined accounting domain, such as a field, a district, or a utility service area. Following \citet{perry2007} and \citet{molden1997}, its withdrawal $W$ divides into depletion $D$ and recoverable return flow $R$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:partition}
  W = D + R .
\end{equation}
$R$ is recoverable return flow: seepage, deep percolation, tailwater, leaks, or treated effluent that re-enters a usable river or aquifer. $D$ is depletion, meaning water lost to the rest of the basin. It has three components,
\[
  D \equiv \Cb + \Cn + N ,
\]
where $\Cb$ is beneficial consumption, $\Cn$ is non-beneficial consumption, and $N$ is non-recoverable outflow. For practices that act on precipitation (Section~\ref{sec:levers}), $W$ is replaced by $W + P$, where $P$ is effective precipitation. Beneficial consumption is ET or incorporation that delivers the intended service, such as ET from crops or lawns. Non-beneficial consumption includes soil and canal evaporation and ET from weeds and phreatophytes. Non-recoverable outflow is water that leaves usable reach, such as flow to a saline sink, the ocean, an out-of-basin export, or an aquifer too deep to pump.

\paragraph{Change identity.} A practice moves a use from state 0 (before) to state 1 (after). With $\Delta$ denoting state 1 minus state 0, the balance must still close:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:change}
  \Delta W = \Delta \Cb + \Delta \Cn + \Delta N + \Delta R ,
  \qquad
  \Delta D = \Delta \Cb + \Delta \Cn + \Delta N .
\end{equation}
This identity holds for every lever, including those that leave $W$ unchanged, and for the change between any two states of a use. Point-of-use reporting sees $-\Delta W$; the basin sees $\Delta D$; the gap between the two is the change in return flow, $\Delta R$. In this notation, the levers of Table~\ref{tab:levers} act as follows. Lever~1 (scale) shrinks $W$, $\Cb$, $\Cn$, $N$, and $R$ in proportion. Lever~2 (partition) changes the split among $\Cb$, $\Cn$, $N$, and $R$ and usually lowers $W$. Lever~3 (disposition) acts through the destinations of the freed water in Equation~\eqref{eq:fate} below. Lever~4 (routing) leaves all four terms nearly unchanged and acts only through where and when return flows arrive, in Equation~\eqref{eq:routing} below. Lever~5 (source) applies Equation~\eqref{eq:change} separately to each source, and a change in water quality can reclassify $R$ as $N$ or $N$ as $R$.

\paragraph{Freed water.} For levers 1 and 2, we separate the technical effect of a practice from what is then done with the water. To do so, we add an intermediate state $\tau$. In state $\tau$ the practice is in place and delivers the original activity (lever~2) or the reduced activity (lever~1), but none of the water it frees has yet been put to use. State $\tau$ is a counterfactual, so it need not be observed. Write $\dtau X \equiv X^{\tau} - X^{0}$ for the change in component $X$ from state 0 to state $\tau$. The \emph{freed withdrawal} $F$ and its two parts are then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:freed}
\begin{aligned}
  F     &\equiv -\dtau W , \\
  \Fwet &\equiv -\dtau D = -\big(\dtau \Cb + \dtau \Cn + \dtau N\big) , \\
  \Fdry &\equiv -\dtau R , \\
  F     &= \Fwet + \Fdry .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The last line follows from Equation~\eqref{eq:change} applied to the step from state 0 to state $\tau$. $F$ is the number programs usually report. Every unit of $F$ either reduces depletion (a ``wet'' saving) or removes water that was already coming back (a ``dry'' saving), in the terms of \citet{seckler1996}. A cut in beneficial consumption ($\dtau \Cb < 0$) also means lost service, such as fewer irrigated acres or less lawn.

\paragraph{Conservation fate equation.} The freed water $F$ does not disappear. In the step from state $\tau$ to state 1, it goes to four destinations $i$, in shares $\lambda_i$ that sum to one. Destination $s$ is water left in the source and legally protected, for example as a trust water right. Destination $u$ is \emph{local reuse}: water that the conserving user, the proprietor, puts back to use on more acres, as a fuller supply under proration, or on new crops. Destination $t$ is water transferred to another user, such as new housing or a junior right holder. Destination $b$ is water deliberately banked or recharged. These destinations correspond to the four ``paracommoners'' of \citet{lankford2023}: proprietor, neighbour, nature, and society. Each destination depletes a fraction $d_i$ of the water it receives, so this step adds $F \sum_i \lambda_i d_i$ to depletion. The change in basin depletion over the whole move from state 0 to state 1, and the \emph{efficacy ratio} $\phi$, are therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fate}
  \Delta D \;=\; \dtau D + F \sum_i \lambda_i\, d_i \;=\; -\Fwet \;+\; F \sum_i \lambda_i\, d_i ,
  \qquad
  \phi \;\equiv\; \frac{-\Delta D}{F} .
\end{equation}
We call $-\Delta D$ the \emph{net savings} of a practice: the reduction in basin depletion once the freed water has reached its destinations. $\phi$ is the share of the reported saving that becomes net savings. It equals one only when the whole saving is wet and the program protects all of it. It is near zero for practices that remove only return flow. It turns negative when consumption by local reuse exceeds the wet saving, which is the irrigation-efficiency paradox \citep{grafton2018}. Net savings is a physical quantity for the basin as a whole. The Washington statutory term ``net water savings'' \citep{rcw9042020} also requires that the water be usable in specified reaches without impairing existing rights; the shortfall measure below addresses that second requirement.

The same two steps give the change in observed withdrawal. If all freed water that is not protected passes through the same diversion, the step from state $\tau$ to state 1 adds $(1-\lambda_s)F$ to withdrawal, so $\Delta W = -F + (1-\lambda_s)F = -\lambda_s F$. Observed withdrawal falls only by the protected share. Under full local reuse, $\Delta W = 0$ while depletion rises by $F d_u - \Fwet$. For this reason, reporting based on withdrawal can miss the paradox entirely.

\paragraph{Where and when.} Effects on third parties, such as lost recharge, lower late-season baseflow, and shifts in timing do not appear in basin totals. For a reach or node $k$ at time $t$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:routing}
  \Delta Q_k(t) \;=\; -\sum_{x} \lambda_s\, \dtau W_x(t)\,\ind[x \to k]
  \;+\; \sum_{j} \big(h_{jk} * \dtau R_j\big)(t)
  \;-\; \sum_{i\neq s} \lambda_i\, d_i\, F(t)\,\ind[i \to k] .
\end{equation}
The first term is protected water that passes node $k$, where $W_x$ is withdrawal at diversion point $x$. This water starts at the diversion point and is available early. The second term is the change in return flow from each return location $j$, negative when return flow falls. It reaches $k$ through a transfer kernel $h_{jk}$ that represents lag and attenuation ($*$ denotes convolution). The third term is new depletion by reuse upstream of $k$. Summed over a full year at a node below every return point, Equation~\eqref{eq:routing} reduces to $-\Delta D$. Between the diversion and the return points, and within the season, it does not. That gap is the timing and location problem: more water early near the diversion, less baseflow late and downstream.

\paragraph{Water availability.} A change in flow matters to other water users and to the environment only where flow is short. For each node $k$ and time $t$, let $\underline{Q}_k(t)$ be the flow needed there, such as an instream flow target or the demands of users who depend on flow at $k$. The shortfall at $k$ and its change from state 0 to state 1 are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:shortfall}
  S_k(t) \;=\; \max\big(0,\ \underline{Q}_k(t) - Q_k(t)\big) ,
  \qquad
  \Delta S_k(t) \;=\; S^1_k(t) - S^0_k(t) ,
\end{equation}
where $Q^1_k(t) = Q^0_k(t) + \Delta Q_k(t)$ from Equation~\eqref{eq:routing}. Where flow exceeds the need in both states, $\Delta S_k(t) = 0$: the change in flow passes by without affecting anyone. Where flow falls short in both states, $\Delta S_k(t) = -\Delta Q_k(t)$, so each unit of lost flow is a unit of lost availability. A practice harms no one downstream if $\Delta S_k(t) \le 0$ at every node and time. This condition is a physical counterpart to the Washington requirement that net water savings not impair existing rights \citep{rcw9042020}. A practice can reduce basin depletion ($\Delta D < 0$) and still fail the condition, if its gains arrive where and when flow is plentiful and its losses arrive where and when flow is short. The need $\underline{Q}_k(t)$ lumps together all demands at a node; dividing a shortfall among individual rights holders requires an allocation rule, such as prior appropriation, which we leave for future work.

\paragraph{Co-effects.} We can also attach to each flow an attribute vector such as heat, salinity, nutrients, or temperature. For example, the loss of evaporative cooling at a site scales with the local ET reduction, $-L_v (\dtau \Cb + \dtau \Cn)$, where $L_v$ is the latent heat of vaporization. Water quality effects follow $\dtau R$ and the loads in that return flow.

\section{The parameters: flavors of consumptive and non-consumptive use}\label{sec:parameters}
% ===========================================================================

``Non-consumptive'' is not one category. Whether removing a return flow matters, and to whom, depends on four properties of that flow. A fifth set of parameters, the disposition shares, determines what the saving becomes. Two consequences follow. First, \emph{the same hardware can belong to different types}. Lining a canal over a saline or unusable aquifer is a wet saving, because seepage there is non-recoverable outflow $N$. Lining a canal over a connected alluvial aquifer, as in Walla Walla, is a dry saving, because seepage there is $R$. Indoor fixtures in an inland city remove effluent that returns to the river. In a coastal city that discharges to the ocean, the same fixtures cut a non-recoverable outflow. \citet{perry2012} notes that municipal and industrial use is mostly non-consumptive, with coastal cities the main exception. Second, \emph{the common split between direct practices (hardware) and indirect ones (pricing, metering, education) is about parameter certainty, not physics}. Tiered rates, metering, and education act on $W$ through behavior. They map onto the same terms through whichever end uses respond: rate signals mostly trim outdoor (consumptive) use, while metering and leak detection mostly trim returns. For indirect practices, $F$ is more uncertain and takes longer to appear, but these practices do not need a separate equation.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Typology: four basic types and four dispositions}\label{sec:typology}
% ===========================================================================

The framework yields four basic types, defined by how the step from state 0 to state $\tau$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:freed} changes the components of the use (Table~\ref{tab:types}). A practice that cuts beneficial consumption is Type A, whatever else it changes, because it trades service for water. Fallowing is Type A even where most of its $F$ is dry, because retiring a use also stops that use's return flow. For practices that keep the service, the larger part of $F$ sets the type: Type B if $\Fwet$ is larger, Type C if $\Fdry$ is larger. Type D practices change timing and location, not volume. A disposition, set by the $\lambda$ shares in Equation~\eqref{eq:fate}, then modifies each type. The type sets the wet part of the saving, the most that can become net savings; the disposition sets how much of that part does.

\midrule
A. Depletion cut, service cut & $\dtau \Cb < 0$ & Baseline depleted share $D/W$; below one because the retired use's returns also stop & Net savings; lost production, lawns and gardens, shade, or ET cooling & Fallowing, deficit irrigation, crop switching, turf removal, outdoor watering limits \\
B. \end{tabularx}

The dispositions modify any type. Under \emph{protection} ($\lambda_s$), the wet part of $F$ becomes net savings; for Types A and B this is the intended outcome. Under \emph{local reuse} ($\lambda_u$), the proprietor puts $F$ back to use, usually with the new, more consumptive technology. Because $d_u$ is high, $\Delta D > 0$ whenever $F \lambda_u d_u > \Fwet$; for Type C, almost any local reuse makes depletion rise \citep{ward2008}. Under \emph{transfer} ($\lambda_t$), the saving supports another use, such as growth or junior rights, and the result depends on $d_t$. A common example is municipal indoor savings that support new housing with outdoor use. Under \emph{banking} ($\lambda_b$), the saving goes into storage or an aquifer for later release, which makes the practice partly Type D. Type C practices and the local-reuse and transfer dispositions are the main routes by which conservation harms third parties; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} returns to this point.

Combining types and dispositions gives a short practice code. Examples are ``C$\cdot$local reuse'' for district modernization with the saving used under proration and ``A$\cdot$protected'' for split-season leasing into trust. A program could report this code next to its point-of-use figure. Figure~\ref{fig:classification} shows how to assign a code. The upper questions use Equation~\eqref{eq:freed} to place a practice in a type; the last uses the $\lambda$ shares in Equation~\eqref{eq:fate} to assign its disposition. Type D practices skip the type questions because they change timing, not volume.

We draw every panel with the same conventions so that readers can compare panels. Every panel has the same total width. A \emph{hydrograph difference plot} shows $\Delta Q_k(t)$ from Equation~\eqref{eq:routing} at two or three named nodes, by month. It displays the early gains and late losses that net to $-\Delta D$. Shading the months in which flow falls short of its need marks the changes that alter availability under Equation~\eqref{eq:shortfall}. A \emph{network schematic} shows diversion points, return points, and receiving users or reaches, with arrows sized by the lost return flow, $-\dtau R$. It identifies who sits between a diversion and its return. The \emph{classification flow} (Figure~\ref{fig:classification}) assigns the practice code. A companion web application draws these displays interactively for one use and one diversion \citep{yoder2026ledger}.

\section{Worked illustrations}\label{sec:examples}
% ===========================================================================

In a stylized district, the conservation option with the largest reported saving delivers the smallest net savings. If the district reuses the freed water locally, the same option raises depletion by 39 units. All numbers are illustrative: they show the arithmetic and are not estimates for any real basin.

\paragraph{Setup.} The district holds a 100-unit diversion. Its unlined canals and surface irrigation split this diversion into crop ET (40), other ET (5), quick tailwater returns (10), and lagged returns (45). The lagged returns are 20 units of canal seepage and 25 of deep percolation, both reaching the river months later through the alluvial aquifer. Baseline depletion is therefore $D = 45$. Modernization (piped canals and sprinklers) serves the same acreage with 50 units: crop ET 40, other ET 2, quick returns 2, and lagged returns 6. Table~\ref{tab:scenarios} summarizes three scenarios.

The scenarios support three points. First, the largest reported saving yields the smallest net savings: modernization reports 50 units but nets 3, while fallowing reports 20 but nets 9. Second, the local-reuse case is the irrigation-efficiency paradox in numbers: in the C$\cdot$local reuse row, $d_u = 42/50$, so $\Delta D = -3 + 50 \times 0.84 = +39$. Third, both modernization scenarios harm third parties, and Equation~\eqref{eq:routing} shows how; basin totals omit these effects. With trust protection, the district puts 52 units in the river near the diversion early in the season, but lagged returns fall from 45 to 6. That late-season water no longer reaches users of downstream baseflow, springs, and wells, as \citet{scherberg2018} modeled for the Walla Walla Basin. Where late-season flow below the return points already falls short of its need, Equation~\eqref{eq:shortfall} converts each unit of that loss into a unit of added shortfall, even though the basin as a whole gains 3 units. With local reuse, depletion rises by 39 units, and every user below the district has less water. Fallowing, by contrast, keeps 36 of the 45 units of late-season return.

\paragraph{Walla Walla.} In framework terms, canal piping in the Walla Walla Basin was Type C. \citet{scherberg2018} modeled the basin and found that piping without MAR reduced groundwater storage. Piping combined with increased MAR allowed higher summer river flows with stable groundwater storage. In framework terms, the lost storage is a third-party effect: it falls on groundwater users and spring-fed reaches that relied on canal seepage. Here MAR, a Type D practice, supplies a deliberate return flow with chosen location and timing ($h_{jk}$), and that return flow replaces the lost seepage.

\paragraph{Municipal.} Each municipal practice maps onto a different term, and two of them can harm third parties. Indoor retrofits in an inland city are Type C: the effluent reaching the river shrinks by almost the full saving, and downstream users or instream flows that relied on that effluent lose it. If the saving supports new connections (transfer), depletion rises by about $d_t F$, where $d_t$ includes the outdoor use of the new homes. The same retrofits in a city that discharges to the ocean are Type B and harm no one downstream. Turf removal is Type A, and its co-effect, which falls on neighbors rather than on water users, follows the cut in $\Cb$. In model simulations for Los Angeles, \citet{vahmani2016} found that replacing lawns raised summer daytime temperatures by up to 1.9\,\textdegree C. In a Phoenix field comparison, \citet{saher2022} found xeric plots about 3\,\textdegree C warmer on average than tree-and-turf plots.

\section{When conservation harms third parties}\label{sec:discussion}
% ===========================================================================

Conservation harms third parties when the conserver's saving comes partly from water that others were already using. The framework identifies five circumstances in which this happens, each with a signal in the equations.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Removing return flow that others rely on.} Type C practices in a basin where return flow is committed to downstream rights or environmental flows: lining or piping canals over a connected aquifer, drip or sprinkler conversion where deep percolation recharged an aquifer, main-line leak repair, and indoor retrofits or effluent reuse in an inland city. The signal is a large $\Fdry$.
  \item \textbf{Consuming the freed water again.} Local reuse under proration or on expanded acreage, and transfers to new connections or junior rights. The signal is $F \sum_i \lambda_i d_i > \Fwet$, so that $\Delta D > 0$ and every user downstream has less water.
  \item \textbf{Shifting water from late to early.} Cutting seepage or percolation that returned slowly moves water from the late season, when flow is usually scarce, to the diversion season. The signal is negative $\Delta Q_k(t)$ in late months, even when $\Delta D \le 0$; long transfer kernels make the shift larger.
  \item \textbf{Moving gains and losses to different places.} Protected water passes the reach between the diversion and the return points, while the loss of return flow falls below the return points and on wells near canals and fields. The signal is $\Delta S_k(t) > 0$ at nodes below the return points.
  \item \textbf{Reducing dilution or cooling.} Smaller return flows can concentrate salts and nutrients and raise stream temperatures, and less outdoor ET raises local air temperatures. The signal is in the attribute vector of each flow.
\end{enumerate}

Two features of these circumstances matter for policy. First, legal protection of the freed water does not cure them on its own: a trust water right protects flow in the reach between diversion and return during the diversion season, not late-season baseflow below the return points. Second, deliberate Type D practices can offset them by restoring return flow where and when it was lost, as managed aquifer recharge does in the Walla Walla Basin \citep{scherberg2018}. The no-harm condition in Section~\ref{sec:identity} gives programs a test to apply before they fund a practice, and the practice code flags the types and dispositions that call for that test.

\section{Next steps}\label{sec:next}
% ===========================================================================

The framework becomes useful once analysts assign each practice in a basin its parameters and practice code. Four steps would get there.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Parameter library.} Tabulate the fractions ($\Cb$, $\Cn$, $N$, $R$), return locations, and lag classes for each common Washington practice and hydrogeologic setting. Start from published practice inventories and program records. Use ranges rather than single values so that the equations propagate uncertainty; for example, a Monte Carlo draw over the fractions gives a distribution for $\phi$.
  \item \textbf{Disclosure template.} Ask each program, funding application, and SEPA document to report $F$, $\Fwet$, $\Fdry$, and the $\lambda$ shares. Each should also report the expected sign of $\Delta Q$ at named downstream nodes in early and late season. This template turns the call for full water accounting \citep{grafton2018} into a reporting standard.
  \item \textbf{Basin pilot.} In Walla Walla or Yakima, estimate the transfer kernels $h_{jk}$ from existing groundwater--surface water models, and set the flow needed at named nodes from instream flow rules and water rights records. Use ET remote sensing to check $\dtau \Cb$ and $\dtau \Cn$, and compare the summed portfolio against observed depletion.
  \item \textbf{Interactive tool.} Extend the companion web application \citep{yoder2026ledger}, which computes $\Delta D$, $\phi$, the two-ledger Sankey, and monthly $\Delta Q$ for one use and one diversion, to a network of uses and to the shortfall measure in Equation~\eqref{eq:shortfall}.
\end{enumerate}
One open question needs an early decision: where the accounting domain boundary should lie (district, sub-basin, or Water Resource Inventory Area). Non-recoverable outflow $N$ and the commitment status of $R$ both depend on that boundary.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
% ===========================================================================

A reported conservation saving is a drop in withdrawal, but a river basin gains only the part that would otherwise have been consumed or lost, and only if that part is not consumed again. The framework in this paper traces the difference. It splits any reported saving into wet and dry parts, follows the freed water to its destinations, places the resulting gains and losses in space and time, and compares the resulting flows with the flow needed at each place and time. Four types and four dispositions follow, and two-ledger Sankey diagrams display the result for any practice in any sector.

The framework also shows when conservation harms third parties. The practices with the largest reported savings can be those that remove return flow, and their savings can come from water that downstream users, groundwater pumpers, and streams were already using. Freed water that is consumed again makes the harm larger. Reporting the wet and dry savings, the disposition of the freed water, and the change in late-season flow at named downstream points would make these effects visible before a program funds a practice.
