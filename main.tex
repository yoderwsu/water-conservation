\begin{abstract}
% DRAFT abstract, written from the framework text; revise before submission.
Conservation programs usually measure success as a drop in withdrawal at the point of use. River basins, however, respond to changes in depletion and to the timing and location of return flows. We propose one parameterized framework that links the two. We use a change identity to divide any reported saving into a ``wet'' part that reduces depletion and a ``dry'' part that only removes return flow. We then add a fate equation for what happens to the freed water (protection, rebound, transfer, or banking) and a routing equation that places the resulting gains and losses in space and time. Four basic types of conservation follow from the parameter signatures, and the terms of the framework map directly onto two-ledger Sankey diagrams. In worked examples, the practice with the largest reported saving delivers the least basin water and, under rebound, increases depletion.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
% ===========================================================================

Conservation programs usually count success as a drop in withdrawal at the point of use. A river basin responds to something else. It responds to the change in depletion, meaning water evaporated, transpired, exported, or sent to a sink. It also responds to shifts in return flows across space and time. The drop in withdrawal and the change in depletion can differ in size, and even in sign.

Evidence for this gap comes from basin models, field data, economic theory, and urban climate studies. \citet{ward2008} modeled the Upper Rio Grande and found that subsidies for efficient irrigation can increase depletion. \citet{pfeiffer2014} used panel data from western Kansas and found that conversion to more efficient center pivots increased groundwater extraction. \citet{contor2013} used an economic model to show why this outcome is the general case when irrigators behave rationally, a point \citet{huffaker2003} made earlier on theoretical grounds. \citet{grafton2018}, in a multi-author synthesis, called this pattern the irrigation-efficiency paradox and called for full water accounting. The gap also arises outside agriculture and outside the annual water balance. In the Walla Walla Basin, \citet{scherberg2018} found that piping canals over a connected aquifer reduced modeled groundwater storage unless managed aquifer recharge (MAR) replaced the lost seepage. Replacing urban lawns reduces consumptive use but raises daytime temperatures \citep{vahmani2016,saher2022}.

Washington law already draws the key distinction, but practice is less settled. The law defines ``net water savings'' as water conserved and usable in specified reaches without impairing existing rights \citep{rcw9042020}. A recent review of conservation in eastern Washington \citep{wadraft2026} found that programs mostly estimate savings at the point of use, report them in aggregate, and seldom trace them to the source. The review also found that agricultural and municipal programs run overlapping practices without coordinating on basin outcomes.

The concepts needed to close this gap exist, but they are spread across separate literatures. These include water-accounting fractions \citep{perry2007,molden1997,karimi2013}, the distinction between ``wet'' and ``dry'' savings \citep{seckler1996}, scale dependence \citep{lankford2020}, and the paracommons, which concerns who gains from a saving \citep{lankford2013,lankford2023}. This paper joins them in one parameterized identity. The identity links the effect of a practice at the point of use to basin depletion and to the timing and location of flows. It applies to any practice in any sector. Basic types follow from its specifications, and its terms map directly onto Sankey diagrams. Section~\ref{sec:contribution} compares the framework with the closest published work.

\section{How a practice can change the fate of water}\label{sec:levers}
% ===========================================================================

A conservation practice can change the fate of water through five levers (Table~\ref{tab:levers}). Some levers do not show up as a drop in withdrawal. Most real practices pull more than one lever at once, and we build the framework in Section~\ref{sec:identity} to track all five.

Two consequences shape the equations that follow. First, \emph{observed withdrawal is an unreliable indicator}. $W$ can fall with no change in depletion, when lever~2 removes only $R$. $W$ can also stay flat while depletion rises, when rebound under lever~3 follows lever~2. The equations therefore track the change in every component, not $W$ alone. Second, \emph{some practices act on water that is never withdrawn}. Rainwater harvesting, cover crops, and land-cover change alter evapotranspiration (ET) of precipitation. The framework handles them by replacing $W$ with total input $W + P$ throughout, where $P$ is effective precipitation. This substitution matches the distinction in basin water accounting between ET supplied by precipitation and incremental ET supplied by withdrawals \citep{karimi2013}.

\section{The foundational identity}\label{sec:identity}
% ===========================================================================

Any practice changes the components of the water balance of a use. Equation~\eqref{eq:partition} partitions a use. Equations~\eqref{eq:change} and~\eqref{eq:freed} account for the change a practice makes and separate the part the basin feels from the part it does not. Equation~\eqref{eq:fate} adds what happens to freed water, and Equation~\eqref{eq:routing} places the effects in space and time.

\paragraph{Partition of use.} Take one use within a defined accounting domain, such as a field, a district, or a utility service area. Following \citet{perry2007} and \citet{molden1997}, its withdrawal $W$ divides into depletion $D$ and recoverable return flow $R$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:partition}
  W = D + R .
\end{equation}
$R$ is recoverable return flow: seepage, deep percolation, tailwater, leaks, or treated effluent that re-enters a usable river or aquifer. $D$ is depletion, meaning water lost to the rest of the basin. It has three components,
\[
  D \equiv \Cb + \Cn + N ,
\]
where $\Cb$ is beneficial consumption, $\Cn$ is non-beneficial consumption, and $N$ is non-recoverable outflow. Beneficial consumption is ET or incorporation that delivers the intended service, such as ET from crops or lawns. Non-beneficial consumption includes soil and canal evaporation and ET from weeds and phreatophytes. Non-recoverable outflow is water that leaves usable reach, such as flow to a saline sink, the ocean, an out-of-basin export, or an aquifer too deep to pump.

\paragraph{Change identity.} A practice moves a use from state 0 (before) to state 1 (after). With $\Delta$ denoting state 1 minus state 0, the balance must still close:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:change}
  \Delta W = \Delta \Cb + \Delta \Cn + \Delta N + \Delta R ,
  \qquad
  \Delta D = \Delta \Cb + \Delta \Cn + \Delta N .
\end{equation}
This identity holds for every lever, including those that leave $W$ unchanged, and for the change between any two states of a use. Point-of-use reporting sees $-\Delta W$; the basin sees $\Delta D$; the gap between the two is the change in return flow, $\Delta R$. Lever~4 (routing) leaves all four terms nearly unchanged and acts only through Equation~\eqref{eq:routing}. Lever~5 (source) applies Equation~\eqref{eq:change} separately to each source.

\paragraph{Freed water.} For levers 1 and 2, we separate the technical effect of a practice from what is then done with the water. To do so, we add an intermediate state $\tau$. In state $\tau$ the practice is in place and delivers the original activity (lever~2) or the reduced activity (lever~1), but none of the water it frees has yet been put to use. State $\tau$ is a counterfactual, so it need not be observed. Write $\dtau X \equiv X^{\tau} - X^{0}$ for the change in component $X$ from state 0 to state $\tau$. The \emph{freed withdrawal} $F$ and its two parts are then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:freed}
  F \;\equiv\; -\dtau W ,
  \qquad
  \Fwet \;\equiv\; -\dtau D \;=\; -\big(\dtau \Cb + \dtau \Cn + \dtau N\big) ,
  \qquad
  \Fdry \;\equiv\; -\dtau R .
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:change}, applied to the step from state 0 to state $\tau$, gives $F = \Fwet + \Fdry$. $F$ is the number programs usually report. Every unit of $F$ either reduces depletion (a ``wet'' saving) or removes water that was already coming back (a ``dry'' saving), in the terms of \citet{seckler1996}. A cut in beneficial consumption ($\dtau \Cb < 0$) also means lost service, such as fewer irrigated acres or less lawn.

\paragraph{Conservation fate equation.} The freed water $F$ does not disappear. In the step from state $\tau$ to state 1, it goes to four destinations $i$, in shares $\lambda_i$ that sum to one. Destination $s$ is water left in the source and legally protected, for example as a trust water right. Destination $u$ is water the proprietor re-applies to more acres, a fuller supply under proration, or new crops. Destination $t$ is water transferred to another user, such as new housing or a junior right holder. Destination $b$ is water deliberately banked or recharged. These destinations correspond to the four ``paracommoners'' of \citet{lankford2023}: proprietor, neighbour, nature, and society. Each destination depletes a fraction $d_i$ of the water it receives, so this step adds $F \sum_i \lambda_i d_i$ to depletion. The change in basin depletion over the whole move from state 0 to state 1, and the \emph{efficacy ratio} $\phi$, are therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fate}
  \Delta D \;=\; \dtau D + F \sum_i \lambda_i\, d_i \;=\; -\Fwet \;+\; F \sum_i \lambda_i\, d_i ,
  \qquad
  \phi \;\equiv\; \frac{-\Delta D}{F} .
\end{equation}
$\phi$ is the share of the reported saving that becomes real water for the basin. It equals one only when the whole saving is wet and the program protects all of it. It is near zero for practices that remove only return flow. It turns negative when rebound consumption exceeds the wet saving, which is the irrigation-efficiency paradox \citep{grafton2018}.

The same two steps give the change in observed withdrawal. If all freed water that is not protected passes through the same diversion, the step from state $\tau$ to state 1 adds $(1-\lambda_s)F$ to withdrawal, so $\Delta W = -F + (1-\lambda_s)F = -\lambda_s F$. Observed withdrawal falls only by the protected share. Under full rebound, $\Delta W = 0$ while depletion rises by $F d_u - \Fwet$. For this reason, reporting based on withdrawal can miss the paradox entirely.

\paragraph{Where and when.} Third-party effects such as lost recharge, lower late-season baseflow, and shifts in timing do not appear in basin totals. For a reach or node $k$ at time $t$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:routing}
  \Delta Q_k(t) \;=\; -\sum_{x} \lambda_s\, \dtau W_x(t)\,\ind[x \to k]
  \;+\; \sum_{j} \big(h_{jk} * \dtau R_j\big)(t)
  \;-\; \sum_{i\neq s} \lambda_i\, d_i\, F(t)\,\ind[i \to k] .
\end{equation}
The first term is protected water that passes node $k$, where $W_x$ is withdrawal at diversion point $x$. This water starts at the diversion point and is available early. The second term is the change in return flow from each return location $j$, negative when return flow falls. It reaches $k$ through a transfer kernel $h_{jk}$ that represents lag and attenuation ($*$ denotes convolution). The third term is new depletion by reuse upstream of $k$. Summed over a full year at a node below every return point, Equation~\eqref{eq:routing} reduces to $-\Delta D$. Between the diversion and the return points, and within the season, it does not. That gap is the timing and location problem: more water early near the diversion, less baseflow late and downstream.

\paragraph{Co-effects.} We can also attach to each flow an attribute vector such as heat, salinity, nutrients, or temperature. For example, the loss of evaporative cooling at a site scales with the local ET reduction, $-L_v (\dtau \Cb + \dtau \Cn)$, where $L_v$ is the latent heat of vaporization. Water quality effects follow $\dtau R$ and the loads in that return flow.

\section{The parameters: flavors of consumptive and non-consumptive use}\label{sec:parameters}
% ===========================================================================

``Non-consumptive'' is not one category. Whether removing a return flow matters, and to whom, depends on four properties of that flow. A fifth set of parameters, the disposition shares, determines what the saving becomes. Each property has a slot in the equations above, so specifying a practice means filling in Table~\ref{tab:parameters}.

Two consequences follow. First, \emph{the same hardware can belong to different types}. Lining a canal over a saline or unusable aquifer is a wet saving, because seepage there is $N$. Lining a canal over a connected alluvial aquifer, as in Walla Walla, is a dry saving, because seepage there is $R$. Indoor fixtures in an inland city remove effluent that returns to the river. In a coastal city that discharges to the ocean, the same fixtures cut a non-recoverable outflow. \citet{perry2012} notes that municipal and industrial use is mostly non-consumptive, with coastal cities the main exception. Second, \emph{the common split between direct practices (hardware) and indirect ones (pricing, metering, education) is about parameter certainty, not physics}. Tiered rates, metering, and education act on $W$ through behavior. They map onto the same terms through whichever end uses respond: rate signals mostly trim outdoor (consumptive) use, while metering and leak detection mostly trim returns. For indirect practices, $F$ is more uncertain and takes longer to appear, but these practices do not need a separate equation.

\section{Typology: four basic types and four dispositions}\label{sec:typology}
% ===========================================================================

The framework yields four basic types, defined by how the step from state 0 to state $\tau$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:freed} changes the components of the use (Table~\ref{tab:types}). A practice that cuts beneficial consumption is Type A, whatever else it changes, because it trades service for water. Fallowing is Type A even where most of its $F$ is dry, because retiring a use also stops that use's return flow. For practices that keep the service, the larger part of $F$ sets the type: Type B if $\Fwet$ is larger, Type C if $\Fdry$ is larger. Type D practices change timing and location, not volume. A disposition, set by the $\lambda$ shares in Equation~\eqref{eq:fate}, then modifies each type. The type determines how much of the saving is real; the disposition determines whether that real part survives.

\midrule
A. B. The dispositions modify any type. Under \emph{protection} ($\lambda_s$), the wet part of $F$ becomes real water for the basin; for Types A and B this is the intended outcome. Under \emph{rebound} ($\lambda_u$), the proprietor re-applies $F$, usually with the new, more consumptive technology. Because $d_u$ is high, $\Delta D > 0$ whenever $F \lambda_u d_u > \Fwet$; for Type C, almost any rebound makes depletion rise \citep{ward2008}. Under \emph{transfer} ($\lambda_t$), the saving supports another use, such as growth or junior rights, and the result depends on $d_t$. A common example is municipal indoor savings that support new housing with outdoor use. Under \emph{banking} ($\lambda_b$), the saving goes into storage or an aquifer for later release, which makes the practice partly Type D.

Combining types and dispositions gives a short practice code. Examples are ``C$\cdot$rebound'' for district modernization with proration reuse and ``A$\cdot$protected'' for split-season leasing into trust. A program could report this code next to its point-of-use figure. Figure~\ref{fig:classification} shows how to assign a code. The upper questions use Equation~\eqref{eq:freed} to place a practice in a type; the last uses the $\lambda$ shares in Equation~\eqref{eq:fate} to assign its disposition. Type D practices skip the type questions because they change timing, not volume.

A two-ledger Sankey diagram draws Equations~\eqref{eq:freed} and~\eqref{eq:fate} directly (Figure~\ref{fig:sankey}). The left column shows what managers measure at the point of use, and the right column shows what the basin receives. The links between them show each $F$ term moving from one fate to another. Across the panels, the fallowing case keeps most of the late-season return. The trust-protected modernization case adds 50 units to the early river but leaves only 6 to arrive late. The rebound case nearly doubles depletion while reporting the same 50-unit saving.

It identifies who sits between a diversion and its return. The \emph{classification flow} (Figure~\ref{fig:classification}) assigns the practice code.

\section{Worked illustrations}\label{sec:examples}
% ===========================================================================

In a stylized district, the conservation option with the largest reported saving delivers the least real water. If the district re-applies the freed water, the same option raises depletion by 39 units. All numbers are illustrative: they show the arithmetic and are not estimates for any real basin.

\paragraph{Setup.} The district holds a 100-unit diversion. Its unlined canals and surface irrigation split this diversion into crop ET (40), other ET (5), quick tailwater returns (10), and lagged returns (45). The lagged returns are 20 units of canal seepage and 25 of deep percolation, both reaching the river months later through the alluvial aquifer. Baseline depletion is therefore $D = 45$. Modernization (piped canals and sprinklers) serves the same acreage with 50 units: crop ET 40, other ET 2, quick returns 2, and lagged returns 6. Table~\ref{tab:scenarios} summarizes three scenarios.

\begin{tabular}{@{}llrrrrrr@{}}

Scenario & Code & $F$ & $\Fwet$ & $\Fdry$ & $\Delta D$ & $\phi$ & Late return \\
\midrule
Fallow 20\%, water into trust & A$\cdot$protected & 20 & 9 & 11 & $-9$ & 0.45 & 36 \\

The scenarios support three points. First, the largest reported saving yields the least real water: modernization reports 50 units but saves 3, while fallowing reports 20 but saves 9. Second, the rebound case is the irrigation-efficiency paradox in numbers: in the C$\cdot$rebound row, $d_u = 42/50$, so $\Delta D = -3 + 50 \times 0.84 = +39$. Third, Equation~\eqref{eq:routing} identifies effects that basin totals omit. Modernization with trust protection puts 52 units in the river near the diversion early in the season, while lagged returns fall from 45 to 6. Downstream baseflow and wells absorb that loss, as \citet{scherberg2018} modeled for the Walla Walla Basin.

\paragraph{Walla Walla.} In framework terms, canal piping in the Walla Walla Basin was Type C. \citet{scherberg2018} modeled the basin and found that piping without MAR reduced groundwater storage. Piping combined with increased MAR allowed higher summer river flows with stable groundwater storage. Here MAR, a Type D practice, supplies a deliberate return flow with chosen location and timing ($h_{jk}$), and that return flow replaces the lost seepage.

\paragraph{Municipal.} Each municipal practice maps onto a different term. Indoor retrofits in an inland city are Type C: the effluent reaching the river shrinks by almost the full saving. If the saving supports new connections (transfer), depletion rises by about $d_t F$, where $d_t$ includes the outdoor use of the new homes. The same retrofits in a city that discharges to the ocean are Type B. Turf removal is Type A, and its co-effect follows the cut in $\Cb$. In model simulations for Los Angeles, \citet{vahmani2016} found that replacing lawns raised summer daytime temperatures by up to 1.9\,\textdegree C. In a Phoenix field comparison, \citet{saher2022} found xeric plots about 3\,\textdegree C warmer on average than tree-and-turf plots.

\section{Next steps}\label{sec:next}
% ===========================================================================

The framework becomes useful once analysts assign each practice in a basin its parameters and practice code. Four steps would get there.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Parameter library.} Tabulate the fractions ($\Cb$, $\Cn$, $N$, $R$), return locations, and lag classes for each common Washington practice and hydrogeologic setting. Start from published practice inventories and program records. Use ranges rather than single values so that the equations propagate uncertainty; for example, a Monte Carlo draw over the fractions gives a distribution for $\phi$.
  \item \textbf{Disclosure template.} Ask each program, funding application, and SEPA document to report $F$, $\Fwet$, $\Fdry$, and the $\lambda$ shares. Each should also report the expected sign of $\Delta Q$ at named downstream nodes in early and late season. This template turns the call for full water accounting \citep{grafton2018} into a reporting standard.
  \item \textbf{Basin pilot.} In Walla Walla or Yakima, estimate the transfer kernels $h_{jk}$ from existing groundwater--surface water models. Use ET remote sensing to check $\dtau \Cb$ and $\dtau \Cn$, and compare the summed portfolio against observed depletion.
  \item \textbf{Interactive tool.} Build a what-if Sankey in which managers set the practice, the setting, and $\lambda$, and see $\Delta D$, $\phi$, and seasonal $\Delta Q$ update together.
\end{enumerate}
One open question needs an early decision: where the accounting domain boundary should lie (district, sub-basin, or Water Resource Inventory Area). Non-recoverable outflow $N$ and the commitment status of $R$ both depend on that boundary.

A manuscript built on this framework would make five contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A general identity for conservation outcomes} that holds for all five levers and all sectors, with the familiar irrigation results as special cases.
  \item \textbf{A typology defined by parameter signatures rather than technologies}. The typology explains why the same hardware is a wet saving in one setting and a dry saving in another.
  \item \textbf{A sign condition for rebound} against which empirical studies such as \citet{pfeiffer2014} could report results in a common form.
  \item \textbf{An explicit link to third-party effects in time and space}, matching legal tests such as Washington's ``net water savings'' without impairment of existing rights.
  \item \textbf{A reporting and visualization standard} for disclosure in funding applications and environmental review. The standard comprises the practice code, $F$, $\Fwet$, $\Fdry$, $\Delta D$, $\phi$, and the two-ledger Sankey.
\end{enumerate}
